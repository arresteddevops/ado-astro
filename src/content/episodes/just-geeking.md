## Nearly 500 Jokes, All by Hand

Cassidy works on the developer advocacy team at GitHub, and her longest-running side project is a weekly newsletter of links, a practice coding question, a joke, and whatever she and others are up to. It has run for nine and a half years, which Matty, a longtime subscriber, calculates at almost 500 jokes. Avoiding repeats is "a shockingly hard challenge." Her archive lives in Obsidian, and before using a joke she searches it to see whether there has ever been one about frogs (there was, six years ago).

Almost none of it is automated. She keeps a bookmark folder called Put In Newsletter, pastes the contents into a markdown file, and pastes that into her sending software. "I handwrite everything." The coding question is the slow part. She now asks AI for topics relevant to the week, which is how a recording at the start of November produced a question about daylight savings time, but she still writes the question herself, because "AI's still not very good at math."

## Building in Public, Mistakes Included

Cassidy started live-streaming almost ten years ago while working on the Echo at Amazon, where she and her sister showed people how to build skills. She liked that questions from the chat made her teach better and turned into blog post ideas. Matty asks about the vulnerability of getting things wrong on camera, and about the second kind of fear, the experienced person who worries about being exposed as a fraud over a botched kubectl command. Cassidy says she has reached a point where she cares "a little less what people think," and it has turned into "a credibility thing": she built it live in front of you, so she knows what she's talking about. She will also say, with no AI and no notes, that she would need a minute to spin up a Docker container, though she could write `useState` from scratch.

## Open Unless It's Truly Niche

Her default is public, because "if I want it to exist, someone else probably does too." The exception is her YouTube thumbnail generator, which uses her font and her format and, in her words, is not optimized for clicks. It stays on her machine. Her short link generator is open source. Someone once wrote to say a script she open sourced five years earlier was the only one they had found that did one specific thing. Matty adds that even when you can't share the thing you built, you can share how you built it, and Cassidy says she keeps a pile of browser tabs for exactly that, including a post about a person who bought a shack in the woods and only added a kitchen and plumbing as they needed them.

## Keyboards, Pens, and Other Snobbery

Collecting, she says, starts when you cross a threshold, accept that you're a snob, and move on. Keyboards were first, and she has more than one digital typewriter. Lately she has become a "writing utensil snob" after someone gave her a nice pen. She now has a pen rack on her desk and a leather notebook with very specific paper. Matty compares notebooks to backpacks, "the biggest it depends in the world," and the two agree that the research never ends.

## The Microphone Stand and the Marble Strings

The last thing she built purely for fun was a 3D-printed stand for a DJI Mic 2, so she can be a newscaster to her own phone. A coworker suggested a GitHub version, she planned to find time for the modeling she's slow at, and the coworker told her to pop a prompt into GitHub Copilot instead. The result has the GitHub logo and every icon from the site embedded in it. She was "actively angry that AI was able to do it so fast," since she had wanted to be an artist about it. Then there is begleri, which she found through a Reddit post from someone who wanted a hobby to do while standing at a retail job. It is two beads on a string, and she makes her own, with polymer clay cherries and paracord knots. There are at least a dozen around her house, and her toddlers keep taking them.

She says the fun drains when it starts to feel like "a chore and obligation." The early sign is apathy.

## Busier Is Better

Having two young kids made her more efficient. She says she has shipped more side projects since they were born, because a free hour while they sleep goes to something on her list and not to "bed rotting and scrolling." Matty connects it to a line he remembers from Rein, "Oh, you're bad at managing incidents. Have you tried having more of them?" With 45 minutes before your next meeting, you get it done. Cassidy agrees, and describes her own version: an hour before the kids wake up, two before a pickup, and says some of those hours are spent resting, since the task for the day is sometimes sleep.

## Scaling Labor, Not Replacing It

Matty relays a line from a newsletter he likes: AI should scale your labor, not replace it. Cassidy's answer is that the line moves around. She doesn't like hearing AI music or watching AI video, because she wants art from people who are feeling something and not from "a giant averaging machine." She recently let an AI tool into her email, nervously, and found triaging threads she had left to die was exactly the toil she wanted gone. For code, it depends on whether she cares. Her ten-year-old open source projects are ones where she knows every line, she still reviews carefully, and "I do genuinely like writing code. It's like solving a puzzle." For the thumbnail generator, "I don't care what the code looks like. I just need it to work," so she vibed the whole thing.

## Bot Internets and Time Capsule Korean

What she geeks out about that nobody asks about is a pair of things. One is browsers, built for humans and now used heavily by AI agents, and what happens if there is a bot internet and a human one, given Cloudflare's claim that more than half the internet is bots. The other is how languages are rendered and translated. Her husband is Korean and moved to the United States at age four, so his Korean is frozen in the slang of when his parents left, and relatives in South Korea tell him he sounds like an old man. Matty compares it to the people who show up at Easter Mass and get every response wrong.

The episode ends with Matty noticing her Cubs hoodie, Cassidy describing herself as a Chicago evangelist, and the two agreeing that tavern-style pizza is the next episode.
